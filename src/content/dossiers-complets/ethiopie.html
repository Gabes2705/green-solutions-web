Foreword

This dossier describes Ethiopia from the angle of irrigation water, climate, fertilisers and crops. It is written for the ministries in charge of agriculture and water, the regional bureaus of agriculture and farmers' cooperatives. It answers three questions: Where is water used, for which crops, and in which regions? It then examines what our products can change, and what that can be worth.

Reading the sources. Every figure refers to a source, named in the text and detailed in Annex A. Each source carries a confidence level:

How to read this document

• A: Official statistics or international database (Ethiopian Statistics Service, FAO, World Bank).

• B: Research institute, United Nations agency, or recognised climate model.

• C: Manufacturer statement, official statement reported by the press, trade press, or estimate.

• "bn" means billion; "M" means million; "km³" means billion cubic metres; "t" means tonnes.

• The Ethiopian crop year runs from September to August. "2021/22" means the season from September 2021 to August 2022.

• A "scenario" is a calculation based on the trials cited. It is not a measurement made in Ethiopia.

Eight key points

1. An agricultural country of 135 million people. Agriculture provides 33% of GDP and 60% of jobs (World Bank, 2025). The population grows by 2.6% a year.

2. Cereals grown mostly under rain. Smallholders harvested 32.8 million tonnes of grain on 12.2 million hectares in 2021/22, excluding Tigray (ESS). Maize, teff, wheat and sorghum dominate.

3. Very different climates. Rainfall ranges from 149 mm a year at Semera (Afar) to 2,745 mm at Bonga (South West), 1991-2020 mean (ERA5). The eastern and south-eastern lowlands are arid.

4. The lowlands are warming. At Semera, the mean temperature has risen by 0.33 °C per decade since 1981 (ERA5). The 2020-2023 drought killed about 6.85 million head of livestock (OCHA, March 2023).

5. Irrigation uses only a small part of the potential. 858,160 ha are equipped (FAOSTAT 2023), 16% of the 5.3 million irrigable hectares estimated by IWMI. In the main season, 1.23 million smallholders irrigate 165,111 ha.

6. Fertilisers are imported, and their cost weighs. Nitrogen use rose from 80,503 t in 2005 to 547,262 t in 2023. At international prices, the minimum spending reached $950 M in 2022. Almost everything arrives through the port of Djibouti.

7. The potential water saving is large. In the central scenario, 1.46 km³ a year, 15% of agricultural water. With this water, about 129,201 more hectares could be irrigated. The range is 0.73 to 2.92 km³.

8. Coffee carries exports. Agricultural exports reached $2.26 bn in 2023 (FAOSTAT), including $1.22 bn of coffee. For 2024/25, the coffee authority reports a record $2.65 bn.

2. Ethiopia in figures

Ethiopia is Africa's second country by population: 135.5 million people in 2025 according to the World Bank. The Ethiopian Statistics Service projects 109.4 million people in July 2024, on a different basis; the two sources are quoted without being mixed. 76% of the people live in rural areas.

Agriculture provides 32.8% of GDP and 60.1% of jobs. GDP per person is 933 dollars (2025). Undernourishment still affects 19.9% of the population (2024, FAO via the World Bank).

The territory covers 112.2 million hectares. Arable land occupies 16.4 million, permanent crops (coffee, khat, enset, fruit) 2.06 million and permanent meadows and pastures 20 million. Forests cover 26.8 million hectares (FAOSTAT, 2023).

Livestock is the second pillar of agriculture. FAOSTAT counts in 2024 71.9 million cattle, 41.1 million sheep, 54.0 million goats and 1.66 million camels. The lowlands of Afar, Somali and southern Oromia live mainly from pastoralism.

3. Sunshine, rainfall and climate change

3.1 Sunshine

Global solar radiation ranges from 1,862 kWh/m² a year at Bonga, in the humid and cloudy South West, to 2,361 kWh/m² at Jijiga (PVGIS, 2005-2020 mean). It is a high level everywhere, comparable to southern Spain: Light does not limit crops, water does.

3.2 Rainfall

Altitude shapes the climate. The highlands, above 1,500 m, receive most of the rain: 1,702 mm a year at Bahir Dar, 1,741 mm at Addis Ababa, 2,745 mm at Bonga. The eastern lowlands are arid: 149 mm at Semera, 473 mm at Werer in the Awash valley. These values are those of the ERA5 model for the grid cell of each capital; they can differ from station records.

The calendar matters as much as the total. In the centre and north, the long rains (kiremt) last from June to September and carry the main harvest, called Meher. A short season (belg) falls from February to May in the east and south. In the south-east, two short seasons (gu and deyr) frame long dry periods. Figure 4 shows the gap between rainfall and reference evapotranspiration (ET0), month by month.

At Werer (Middle Awash), reference evapotranspiration reaches 2,198 mm a year for 473 mm of rain: No crop is possible there without irrigation. At Bahir Dar, rain exceeds needs from June to September, but the dry season lasts from November to April.

3.3 Climate change

The ERA5 series from 1981 to 2024 shows clear warming in the lowlands: At Semera, the mean annual temperature rose from 29.7 °C (1981-1990) to 30.9 °C (2015-2024), 0.33 °C per decade. In the highlands, the rise is smaller in the same series: 0.09 °C per decade at Bahir Dar and 0.06 °C at Addis Ababa.

Droughts recur. From late 2020 to early 2023, five consecutive rainy seasons failed in the Horn of Africa. In Ethiopia, the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) counted in March 2023 about 6.85 million dead livestock and about 11 million food-insecure people, mainly in Somali and southern Oromia (A10).

4. Irrigation water: Resources, areas, potential

4.1 Resources and withdrawals

Internal renewable water resources reach 122 km³ a year, 973 m³ per person (World Bank, 2022): Already below the threshold of 1,000 m³ per person often used to define scarcity. Withdrawals total 10.55 km³ a year, of which 91.8% for agriculture, 9.69 km³. Water stress, the ratio of withdrawals to available resources, is 32%.

The country is a regional water tower: The Blue Nile (Abbay) rises at Lake Tana, the Awash flows towards the Afar depression, the Shebelle and the Genale-Dawa run down to Somalia, the Omo to Lake Turkana. The Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD), on the Blue Nile, was inaugurated on 9 September 2025; it is designed for electricity (about 5,150 MW) with a reservoir of about 74 billion m³ (A11).

4.2 Irrigated areas today

FAOSTAT counts 858,160 ha equipped for irrigation in 2023. The Ethiopian Statistics Service measures, for the main season 2021/22 alone, 165,111 ha irrigated by 1,228,082 smallholders, an average of 1,344 m² per farmer (A4). This survey covers neither commercial farms, nor dry-season irrigated wheat, nor the short (belg) season.

The two Hararghe zones, in eastern Oromia, alone irrigate more than a fifth of smallholder areas, mostly for khat and vegetables. Gamo zone, around Arba Minch, irrigates banana groves and orchards.

4.3 Potential and programmes

The International Water Management Institute (IWMI) estimates the irrigable area from rivers, groundwater and rainwater harvesting at 5.3 million hectares, of which 3.7 million from surface water (A9). The 858,160 ha equipped represent 16% of it.

Since 2019, the government has developed dry-season irrigated wheat in the lowlands (Awash, Shebelle, Omo). The Ministry of Agriculture reports a rise from 20,000 ha in 2020 to 3.5 million hectares in 2025 (A12). These areas do not yet appear in ESS surveys, and the estimates of the US agriculture department (USDA) differ (A13). The dossier quotes the official figure without using it in the calculation.

Dividing agricultural water (9.69 km³) by the equipped area gives an average of 11,288 m³ per irrigated hectare per year. It is the order of magnitude of the hot lowlands, where sugar cane, cotton and orchards need a lot of water.

5. Fertilisers: Quantities, purchases, prices, spending

5.1 Quantities used

Mineral nitrogen use rose from 80,503 t in 2005 to 547,262 t in 2023, and phosphate (P₂O₅) reaches 194,069 t. Potash remains almost absent (1,952 t of K₂O). Per hectare of arable land, the average input is 45.4 kg (World Bank, 2024), still below the 50 kg target set by the Abuja Declaration on fertilisers (2006).

The ESS survey details use by smallholders in the 2021/22 main season: 2.06 million tonnes of chemical fertiliser on 9.6 million hectares. The NPS and urea combination dominates (785,206 t), ahead of blended fertilisers used with urea (887,965 t). Teff, maize and wheat receive most of it (A4).

5.2 Purchasing and supply

The State buys fertilisers by tender, through the Ethiopian Agricultural Businesses Corporation (EABC), then distributes them through unions and cooperatives. For the 2025/26 season, the announced target was about 24 million quintals, 2.4 million tonnes; by March 2025, 797,463 t had already been unloaded from 15 ships at the port of Djibouti (A14). Almost all the volume goes through this port, then by road and by the Djibouti-Addis Ababa railway.

In August 2025, Ethiopian Investment Holdings and the Dangote group signed an agreement for a urea plant at Gode (Somali region), with an announced capacity of 3 million tonnes a year, supplied with gas from the Calub and Hilala fields; the investment is estimated at 2.5 billion dollars (A15).

5.3 Prices and spending

International prices doubled in 2021-2022 before falling back: Urea cost 700 $ a tonne in 2022 and 338 $ in 2024. Converting nutrients into urea, TSP and potassium chloride at international prices, the country's minimum spending reached $950 M in 2022 and $630 M in 2023. Prices paid delivered to Ethiopia are higher.

8. Extrapolation by crop and water savings

8.1 Method

Method in brief

• Agricultural water: 9.69 km³ a year (World Bank, 2022), shared between crop groups according to their share of the irrigated area.

• Irrigated area: 858,160 ha equipped (FAOSTAT 2023). Estate sugar cane (23,419 ha harvested) is counted separately; the rest follows the mix of smallholder irrigated crops (ESS 2021/22).

• Water saving: For each group, a quarter (cautious), half (central) or all (strong) of the saving measured in the closest trial. Without a trial, 5, 10 or 20%.

• Yield gain: Same rule, applied to FAOSTAT 2024 average yields and valued at constant 2014-2016 prices.

• What the calculation does not contain: No product price, no application cost.

8.6 Reading the results

Key points

• Maize (417 Mm³) and the onion, garlic and potato group (308 Mm³) give half of the saving, because published trials exist for these crops.

• Khat and coffee occupy a large share of irrigated areas but rely on a generic assumption: A trial in Ethiopia on these crops would change the result strongly.

• Shared according to smallholder irrigation, the central result gives about 652 Mm³ for Oromia, 406 Mm³ for Amhara and 253 Mm³ for the southern regions.

• Dry-season irrigated wheat is not counted. If it occupies the announced areas, the water at stake is much larger.

Ethiopia has no access to the sea. According to the figures published for 2025/26, the ports of Djibouti handled 96.7% of Ethiopia's external freight (A19). Annex B. Calculation method

1. Agricultural water. Water withdrawals for agriculture amount to 9.69 km³ a year: 10.55 km³ of total withdrawals × 91.84% for agriculture (World Bank, 2022).

2. Areas by group. The equipped area (858,160 ha, FAOSTAT 2023) is split in two: Sugar cane harvested by the estates (23,419 ha, FAOSTAT 2024), then the rest according to the mix of smallholder irrigated crops (ESS 2021/22, table 6). This split is an assumption: Commercial farms grow more cane, cotton, fruit and flowers.

3. Water by group. Agricultural water is shared in proportion to areas. The thirstiest crops (cane, bananas) are therefore underestimated, cereals overestimated.

4. Water saving. For each group, the saving measured in the analogous trial is applied at a quarter, half or in full (cautious, central, strong scenarios). Without a trial, 5, 10 or 20%.

5. Yield. The trial's yield gain, applied the same way, multiplies the group's FAOSTAT 2024 average yield and its irrigated area. It is valued at the implicit price at constant 2014-2016 value (FAOSTAT 2023 production value ÷ 2023 production).

6. Extra irrigable hectares. The water saved is divided by today's average use (11,288 m³ per hectare per year).

7. Regions. The national result is shared according to the smallholder irrigated area in 2021/22 (ESS, table 5).

8. Fertilisers. Minimum spending = nitrogen ÷ 0.46 × urea price + phosphate ÷ 0.46 × TSP price + potash ÷ 0.60 × potassium chloride price (World Bank prices).

9. Climate. Rainfall, reference evapotranspiration (FAO-56) and temperature: 1991-2020 means of the ERA5 grid cell containing each capital; radiation: PVGIS, 2005-2020. Temperature trends are linear regressions over 1981-2024.

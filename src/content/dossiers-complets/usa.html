Foreword

This dossier describes the United States from the angle of irrigation water, climate, fertilisers and crops. It is intended for the authorities responsible for agriculture and water, and for the economic players of the sector. It answers three questions: Where is irrigation water used, for which crops, and with what reserves? It then examines what our products can change, and what that may be worth, for the country, for each of the ten farm production regions and for each state.

The dossier rests on the 2023 Irrigation and Water Management Survey of the US Department of Agriculture. For every state, it gives the water applied to each crop, its source, the irrigation methods, the price of purchased water and the energy spent on pumping.

Reading the sources. Every figure refers to a source, named in the text and detailed in Annex A. Each source carries a confidence level:

How to read this document

• A: Official statistics or international database (USDA, US Geological Survey, Bureau of Reclamation, FAO, World Bank, European Commission).

• B: Research institute, university, public body outside statistics, or recognised climate model.

• C: Manufacturer statement, non-independent demonstration, press, or estimate.

• One acre-foot is the water that covers one acre to a depth of one foot: 1,233 m³. One acre is 0.405 hectare.

• « bn » means billion; « M » means million; « af » means acre-feet.

• A « scenario » is a calculation based on the trials cited. It is not a measurement made in the United States.

What this dossier does not do. It does not turn a trial carried out elsewhere into an American result. Water savings and yield gains by crop are scenarios, built from the trials cited.

What remains to be completed. Some figures of the survey are withheld to protect individual farms. Section 12 gives the details.

Seven key points

1. Irrigation feeds a large share of American farming. 53.1 million acres were irrigated in 2023 on 212,714 farms. Four western regions (Pacific, Mountain, Northern and Southern Plains) apply 82% of the water.

2. Groundwater is the first source, and it is being drawn down. Wells supply 54% of the water applied. The High Plains aquifer has lost about 286 million acre-feet since 1950, about 10% of its recoverable water (US Geological Survey).

3. A dry, hot West. From June to August, the atmosphere demands 680 mm more water than the rain brings at Phoenix, and 663 mm at Fresno. In the East, rainfall covers most of the summer demand.

4. The climate is warming and water is being rationed. The contiguous United States has warmed by 2.5 °F (1.4 °C) since 1970. Arizona has been under a declared Colorado River shortage since 2022, with a cut of 512,000 acre-feet a year.

5. The potential water saving is large. In the central scenario, 10.7 million acre-feet per year, 13% of irrigation water. The range runs from 5.3 to 21.3 million acre-feet.

6. Corn and three regions carry the result. Corn for grain gives 21% of the central saving. The Pacific, Mountain and Northern Plains regions give 76%.

7. A major agricultural exporter. The United States exported $172 billion of agricultural products in 2024. Corn, soybeans, almonds, cotton and rice all depend on irrigation water in the regions that grow them.

3.2 Rainfall and summer deficit

The United States receives on average 715 mm of rain per year (World Bank). At the farm stations, rainfall ranges from 249 mm at Phoenix to 1,297 mm at Greenville (1991–2020 average). For agriculture, the decisive figure is the summer gap: From June to August, the atmosphere demands far more water than the rain provides in the West and the Plains.

At Fresno, in the Central Valley of California, almost no rain falls from May to September, while the atmosphere demands more than 200 mm a month from June to August. At Grand Island, in Nebraska, summer storms cover part of the demand. At Stuttgart, in the Arkansas Delta, the rain is abundant but falls in winter and spring; the rice and soybeans still need irrigation in July and August. Keeping water in the soil, close to the roots, reduces the water each irrigation must bring.

3.3 Climate change, aquifers and rivers

The Fifth National Climate Assessment (2023) finds that the contiguous United States has warmed by 2.5 °F (1.4 °C) since 1970, faster than the world as a whole (1.7 °F). Hotter summers raise the water demand of crops and shorten the snowpack that fills western reservoirs.

4.2 Where the water comes from

Wells supply 43.9 million acre-feet (54%). Irrigation districts, federal and state projects and other off-farm suppliers deliver 28.4 million, and farmers draw 8.6 million from their own streams, ponds and reservoirs. The West relies on snowmelt stored behind dams; the Plains and the Delta rely on aquifers.

4.3 Irrigation methods

Sprinklers, mostly centre pivots, water 30.8 million acres. Gravity methods (furrows, flooding, basins) still water 18.3 million acres, mostly rice, alfalfa, pasture and cotton. Drip and micro-irrigation cover 6.4 million acres, mostly orchards, vineyards and vegetables. Our water retainers work with all three methods: They hold the water each pass brings, whatever the way it is applied.

4.4 Water resource regions

Hydrologists divide the country into water resource regions, which follow the large river basins. The California basin applies the most water, ahead of the Missouri basin (Nebraska, Kansas, the Dakotas, Montana, Wyoming, Colorado) and the Pacific Northwest (the Columbia and Snake basins).

4.5 The price of water

There is no single price of irrigation water. Farms that buy their water from a district or a project paid on average $54.22 per acre-foot in 2023, for a total of $1.14 billion. Farms that pump from wells pay the energy: $3.26 billion in 2023, of which $2.53 billion of electricity, for 608,288 pumps. Spread over all the water applied, the energy represents about $40 per acre-foot.

4.6 Interrupted irrigation

In 2023, 2,022,246 irrigated acres had lower yields because irrigation was interrupted. A shortage of surface water was the first cause (850,172 acres), ahead of equipment failures and a shortage of groundwater (510,222 acres). Water kept in the soil gives crops a reserve when a delivery is cut or a well runs short.

5.2 Prices

International fertiliser prices doubled in 2021–2022, then fell back. In 2024, urea was worth 338 dollars a tonne and potassium chloride 295 dollars. The prices paid by farmers, with transport and margins, are higher.

5.3 Indicative spending

Converted into urea, triple superphosphate and potassium chloride, the nutrients used represent spending of at least 14.3 billion dollars in 2024, and 29.3 billion in 2022, the year of the price peak. Nutrients washed below the roots by irrigation or heavy rain are lost to the crop; a water retainer that holds the water in the root zone also holds the nutrients dissolved in it.

Limits of the calculation

• International prices, without transport, duties or margins: Actual spending is higher.

• Compound, organic and foliar fertilisers are not distinguished.

8. Extrapolation by crop and water savings

8.1 Method

Method in brief

• Irrigation water: 80.9 million acre-feet applied in 2023 (USDA), measured crop by crop and state by state in the survey.

• Water saving: For each crop, a quarter (cautious), half (central) or all (strong) of the saving measured in the closest trial. Without a trial, 5%, 10% or 20%.

• Yield gain: Same rule, applied to the national yields of 2023 (FAOSTAT) and valued at the implicit 2023 price (value of production ÷ production).

• Value of water: $54.22, $94.48 or $150 per acre-foot (section 4.5).

• By state and by region: Same calculation, with the irrigated acres and the water per acre of each crop in each state.

• What the calculation does not contain: No product price, no application cost.

Key takeaways

• The central saving (10.7 million acre-feet per year) equals 13% of the irrigation water applied in 2023, or the yearly water of about 7.1 million irrigated acres.

• Corn for grain gives 21% of the result; vegetables and potatoes give 16%. These are the irrigated crops for which published trials exist.

• Alfalfa, orchards, pasture, rice and cotton rest on a generic assumption: A trial in the United States on these crops would change the result strongly.

• The survey gives the water per acre of each crop; the calculation therefore follows the real weight of each crop, state by state.

12. Limits and points to complete

What remains to be completed

• Water by crop: The survey withholds some figures to protect individual farms. When a crop's water per acre is withheld in a state, the calculation uses the national average for that crop.

• Unitemised water: Where the water of the listed crops falls short of the state total, the difference is counted as « other irrigated land » with the generic assumption.

• Climate: Two stations per region, taken from a reanalysis model with a grid of about 9 km; they do not describe a whole region.

• Price of water: Three reference values frame the average price of purchased water; water rights and drought-year market prices in the West are not priced.

• Fertilisers: International prices, not the prices paid by American farmers.

• Trials: No trial of our products has yet been published on irrigated crops in the United States; all results are scenarios.

Annex B. Calculation method

Radiation. Sum of the monthly horizontal radiation values from PVGIS, divided by the number of years, at each of the twenty stations.

Rainfall and evapotranspiration. 1991–2020 averages of the daily ERA5-Land values at each station; summer deficit = reference evapotranspiration minus rainfall, June to August.

Irrigation water. Water applied in the open from all sources in 2023 (USDA IWMS, table 7): 80,937,415 acre-feet nationally, and the same figure for each state.

Water by crop. Irrigated acres harvested × average acre-feet applied per acre, for each crop and each state (table 39). Vegetables = all vegetables minus potatoes. When a state's water per acre is withheld, the national value for the crop is used. Water not itemised = state total minus the sum of the crops.

Water saved. Water of the crop × water saving of the scenario.

Yield gain. Irrigated acres × 0.405 × national yield 2023 (FAOSTAT) × gain of the scenario.

Value created. Water saved in acre-feet × value of water ($54.22, $94.48 or $150) + yield gain × implicit 2023 price (value of production ÷ production).

Scenarios. Cautious: 25% of the trial effect. Central: 50%. Strong: Published effect. Crops without a trial: 5%, 10% and 20% water saving, without yield gain.

Regions. Sum of the states of each USDA farm production region; Alaska and Hawaii are counted in the Pacific region.

Fertiliser spending. Nitrogen ÷ 0.46 × urea price + phosphate ÷ 0.46 × TSP price + potash ÷ 0.60 × potassium chloride price, at World Bank international prices. It is a lower bound.

General limits. One coefficient per crop; national yields and prices; no product cost is included in the calculations of the dossier.
